*For a young woman beginning to discover her sensitivity—and anyone who has ever wondered, “Why do I feel so much?”*

**Am I psychic or an empath?**

An empath is commonly understood as someone who feels especially responsive to other people’s emotions. In spiritual practice, the word may also describe an intuitive sensitivity to those feelings. Empathy and psychic perception are different concepts, however, and being sensitive does not by itself establish psychic ability. Understanding your experiences begins with observation, discernment, and learning what belongs to you.

Have you ever spent time with someone and left feeling completely different from when you arrived? Perhaps they said everything was fine, but you sensed sadness. Perhaps a tense conversation stayed with you long after everyone else appeared to have forgotten it.

You may find yourself wondering whether you are unusually sensitive, highly observant, intuitive—or experiencing something you would describe as psychic.

Those questions deserve more than “Yes, you’re an empath. Here are twenty signs.”

Let me begin with something I wish I had understood as a little girl: another person’s feelings are not necessarily about you.

## What is an empath?

In everyday language, an empath is someone who feels particularly affected by the emotional experiences of others. They may describe understanding another person’s sadness almost as though they were feeling it themselves.

Psychology distinguishes several related processes. Empathy involves understanding or sharing another person’s experience; emotional contagion describes how emotions can spread between people. These experiences do not require a paranormal explanation.

In spiritual conversations, *empath* often carries an additional meaning: someone who believes they intuitively perceive emotional information beyond what has been openly communicated.

It helps to establish which meaning you are discussing. Feeling deeply, noticing subtle behavior, and receiving what you interpret as a psychic impression are not automatically the same experience.

## How did my own sensitivity begin?

As a child, I was extraordinarily sensitive to other people’s emotions. The confusing part was that I did not always distinguish what I felt from what they felt. If someone was troubled or angry, I could assume that their feelings were directed toward me.

I did not have a useful vocabulary for it. I was a little girl trying to understand why the people around me affected me so strongly.

Over time, I learned that someone else’s sadness did not have to become my sadness, and another person’s anger was not automatically my fault. Today, I understand those early experiences as empathic sensitivity. You can read more about that journey in [my story](/meet).

That distinction—*what am I feeling, and what am I assuming it means?*—is still worth making.

## Does being an empath mean you are psychic?

Not necessarily. You can be compassionate, emotionally perceptive, and deeply affected by others without interpreting those qualities as psychic.

In my own spiritual practice, I distinguish empathic sensitivity from clairvoyance and clairaudience. Empathic sensitivity concerns feelings. Clairvoyance describes the visual impressions I receive, while clairaudience describes information I hear.

These experiences can work together in my readings, but one does not automatically establish the others.

Nor do you have to decide on a label immediately. “I noticed something I don’t yet understand” is a perfectly reasonable place to begin. You do not need business cards by Friday.

## How might you first notice your sensitivity?

It may begin with an experience that makes you pause: recognizing that a friend is troubled before they speak, having a vivid dream that later seems relevant, or experiencing an impression you cannot immediately explain.

The important word is *notice*. What happened before you decided what it meant?

Suppose you feel uneasy around someone. You may have registered their expression, tone, or behavior without consciously identifying it. Your own memories or worries may also contribute. You might understand the experience spiritually, but the strength of the feeling alone cannot tell you its source.

Curiosity gives you room to explore. An immediate conclusion can close that room surprisingly quickly.

## How can you tell your feelings from your interpretation?

Begin by separating three things:

| What happened | What you experienced | What you concluded |
|---|---|---|
| A friend answered briefly. | You felt a sudden uneasiness. | “She is angry with me.” |
| You entered a busy gathering. | You felt overwhelmed. | “I am absorbing everyone’s emotions.” |
| Someone came to mind unexpectedly. | You felt a strong urge to contact them. | “They must need me.” |

The experience may be real and powerful while the conclusion still needs checking.

Your friend might be distracted. The gathering might simply be noisy and tiring. The person who came to mind might appreciate a hello without being in difficulty.

This does not require dismissing your sensitivity. It means giving yourself a chance to interpret it carefully.

## What should you do with an impression?

When appropriate, ask a simple, respectful question.

“You seem a little quiet. How are you?” leaves someone room to tell you. “I know you’re upset, and I know why” gives them considerably less room.

Even when you feel confident, the other person remains the authority on what they choose to share. If your interpretation does not fit, listen. Do not turn their disagreement into evidence that they are hiding something.

Sensitivity becomes more useful when it supports understanding rather than replacing conversation.

## How can you explore your experiences without getting carried away?

Keep a record before you know the outcome. Write down the impression, its date, the circumstances, and what you thought it meant. Later, record what happened—including the occasions when it did not match.

Be specific. “Something will happen soon” is difficult to evaluate. An exact note gives you a better opportunity to distinguish an impression from what you added afterward.

I would also note your own state. Were you tired, worried about someone, or hoping strongly for a particular answer? That context matters when you look back.

There is no benefit in giving yourself full credit for every apparent success while quietly forgetting the rest. Discernment takes honesty.

## Do you have to feel everything to care about someone?

No. Compassion does not require you to carry every difficult feeling into the rest of your day.

You can listen without taking responsibility for solving someone’s entire life. You can care and still end a conversation when you need rest. You can recognize that a person is distressed without deciding you must remain available around the clock.

Practical boundaries might include taking quiet time after an intense conversation, limiting how much distressing material you consume, or saying, “I care about this, but I don’t have the capacity for a long conversation tonight.”

Being sensitive does not appoint you the emotional emergency department for everyone you know.

## Can you develop sensitivity into something useful?

You can develop habits that make your responses more thoughtful: attentive listening, checking assumptions, keeping records, respecting privacy, and learning when to pause.

If spiritual exploration interests you, approach it patiently. In my own journey, years of numerology study preceded my work with Tarot. The cards’ symbolism helped bring what I understand as clairvoyance and clairaudience into clearer expression. It was a process of study and experience, not an instant professional identity.

You do not need to follow my route. You also do not need to turn every private experience into a service for other people.

There is value in becoming someone who listens well, notices carefully, and responds with kindness. That is already useful.

## What if the experiences feel overwhelming?

You are allowed to step back. You do not have to intensify an experience, practice constantly, or interpret everything spiritually to take your curiosity seriously.

If what you are experiencing becomes frightening, disrupts sleep, or makes ordinary life difficult, speak with a qualified healthcare professional. Seeking support does not require you to abandon your spiritual beliefs.

Exploration should leave room for your wellbeing, your relationships, and the rest of your life.

## You don’t have to understand everything today

When I look back at the sensitive little girl I was, I wish she had known she could feel something deeply without immediately making it her responsibility.

That is where I would encourage you to begin. Notice what happens. Give yourself time to understand it. Ask questions, check your interpretations, and allow yourself to be wrong without treating it as a personal failure.
